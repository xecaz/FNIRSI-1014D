00The one irreversible thing

Read this before you plug anything in.

Stop — unrecoverable

Your scope's per-unit calibration lives at flash offset 0x1FD000. It is written at the factory against your analog front end. There is no download for it and no way to regenerate it at home. Somebody else's dump is not a spare copy of yours.

Everything else on the chip is recoverable, because everything else is the same on every unit with the same firmware. So the whole procedure below reduces to one rule: get a verified full-chip backup before the first write, and the worst case stops being a brick and starts being an inconvenience.

Measured, and mildly surprising

That region is calibration and live configuration. The firmware rewrites part of it during ordinary use — on the bench, 0x1FD014 went 0x04 → 0x03 across one normal boot with nothing written by us.

So two dumps of your own scope will differ there. A one-byte diff at 0x1FD0xx after a power cycle is the scope saving a setting, not evidence of a stray write. Know that before it scares you.

01What's on the chip

Four Allwinner eGON blocks near the start, calibration in the last few kilobytes, and about a megabyte of nothing in between — which turns out to be useful.

OffsetSize (v3.0)MagicContentsChecksum field
0x0000000x3400eGON.BT0SPL — draws the splash, starts the appvalid sunxi sum
0x0060000xBA00eGON.EXE1st executable — FEL helper0x00000000
0x0130000x13000eGON.BMPBoot splash — the targetnot a sum

This guide used to get this wrong

An earlier version stated that the splash block is identical across models — same 0xE600, same 298 × 98. It is not. The block varies by firmware version, not by model.

A 1014D running v3.0 carries a 0x13000-byte block holding a 298 × 130 image; the extra 32 rows are the "Firmware version: V3.0" line drawn into the logo. Upstream's published 1014D dump is an older build: 298 × 98 in 0xE600. The two differ in 1,566,094 of 2,097,152 bytes.

Only the offset 0x013000 and the pixel format are stable. Read geometry from the block header, which is what fnirsi_splash.py does — and why it read a v3.0 scope correctly with no changes.

On checksums

The eGON.BT0 field is a real, standard sunxi checksum — sum of 32-bit words with the field itself replaced by the stamp 0x5F0A6C39 — and it verifies on genuine dumps, on upstream's fel-sdboot.sunxi, and on a patched SPL that boots. The two .EXE blocks and the .BMP field match no sum over any range tried.

A patched bitmap with a stale .BMP checksum boots fine. That is now confirmed on hardware, twice. Leave the field alone, which is the tool's default.

02The splash format

No compression, no palette, no encryption. Raw framebuffer bytes.

The SPL reads width from header 0x1A and height from 0x1C, then centres the image with (800−w)/2, (480−h)/2. So the stock splash is a small banner on a much larger screen — that is FNIRSI's design, not a defect, and your replacement occupies exactly the space theirs did unless you follow §06.

Verified on hardware

Extract to PNG, re-encode, reinsert — reproduces the source image byte for byte, on three different dumps now. And a replaced splash read back off the chip matched the intended file across all 2,097,152 bytes.

03What you need

  • The USB-A to USB-A cable the scope shipped with. That odd cable is the whole story: the scope's port is a device port wired to the F1C100s.
  • sunxi-fel built with SPI flash support. Stock distro packages often lack it. A prebuilt copy ships upstream under Binaries/Hacked files/sunxi_stuff/, and it runs fine on x86-64.
  • fel-sdboot.sunxi, from the same directory.
  • This project's fnirsi_splash.py, backup-scope.sh, and build-sd-wipe.py.
You do not need a microSD card

An earlier version of this guide opened with "write the stub to a microSD and insert it." The 1014D has no card slot. That instruction was inherited from 1013D material and is wrong for this scope.

There is a card — it is internal, it holds your saved screenshots, and the boot ROM checks it before SPI flash. You reach it over USB. See §04.

You do not need to open the scope and you do not need a SOIC clip.

04Getting into FEL — and back out

The way in is easy. The way out is the part nobody warns you about, so it is documented here first.

In: the internal card, over USB

Boot the scope and open its USB connection screen. It enumerates as 0483:5720 — ST's example IDs, which the firmware reuses verbatim — with SCSI vendor ADS1014D. That disk is the SoC's MMC0, and the boot ROM reads it before SPI flash.

Confirm the node before you write to it
$ lsblk -o NAME,SIZE,TRAN,VENDOR,MODEL
sdb   7.5G usb  ADS1014D  USB DisK Disk

# byte 8192 = sector 16. The FAT32 partition starts at sector 63 = byte 32256.
$ udisksctl unmount -b /dev/sdX1
$ sudo dd if=fel-sdboot.sunxi of=/dev/sdX bs=1024 seek=8 conv=fsync

# power-cycle the scope, then:
$ lsusb | grep 1f3a:efe8
Bus 001 Device 033: ID 1f3a:efe8 Allwinner Technology sunxi SoC OTG connector in FEL mode
Check twice

/dev/sdX is a placeholder. Confirm the ADS1014D vendor string and the size before pressing enter. The 8 KiB stub lands in zeroed unallocated space with 15,872 bytes to spare before the filesystem, so your saved captures are untouched — but only if you hit the right disk.

The scope now comes up with a blank screen. That is FEL succeeding, not a fault: the SoC is sitting in USB recovery with no firmware running to draw anything.

Out: the trap

Read this before you write the stub

The stub is self-perpetuating. FEL never starts the scope application — and the application is the only thing that serves that card over USB. You cannot undo the write the way you made it.

Plan for this before you create the situation, not after.

Booting the app out of FEL does not work. These were all tried on real hardware and all failed:

AttemptResult
sunxi-fel spl <stock SPL>Draws the splash, then hands control back to FEL — spl patches an SPL to return
write 0x0 <spl> exe 0x0ERROR -1. FEL's exception vectors live in SRAM at 0x0
write 0x7FFFFFE0 <app> exeDRAM is not initialised in a bare FEL — readl 0x80000000 returns zeros
peco bootloader → SPI appLoads and jumps; black screen. Unchanged with caches disabled
peco bootloader → 0xFFFF0020DRAM comes up, FEL does not re-enter cleanly; screen fills with framebuffer noise

What works is not booting the scope at all. Run code that writes to the card directly. build-sd-wipe.py takes upstream's fnirsi_1014d_startup bootloader — which already brings up clocks, DRAM, display and the SD card, and links in sd_card_write() — and replaces its dead SD-boot path with about a hundred bytes that zero sector 16 for 16 blocks.

The way out — screen goes green on success, red on failure
$ python3 build-sd-wipe.py work/peco-bootloader.bin work/sd-wipe.bin
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel spl work/sd-wipe.bin

Power-cycle. The boot ROM finds nothing on the card, falls through to SPI flash, and the scope boots normally.

Two sunxi-fel behaviours that will waste your evening

A FEL session is effectively single-use. After any spiflash-write or spl, the next bulk transfer fails with usb_bulk_send() ERROR -7: Operation timed out. Power-cycle between every operation. This is not a failure and not your cable.

After running code that never returns, ERROR -7 is the expected result. Judge by the scope's screen, never the terminal. And lsusb caches: a running app that isn't driving USB leaves the stale FEL entry listed, so lsusb cannot tell you whether the app started.

05The procedure

Ordered by risk: everything that protects you happens before anything that changes the scope.

Step 1 — Back up the whole chip, twice

Two independent reads compared against each other. If they differ, the read is unreliable and you must not proceed — a corrupt backup is worse than none, because you will trust it.

backup-scope.sh does all of this and refuses to continue on a mismatch
$ FEL=./work/sunxi-fel ./backup-scope.sh

# or by hand:
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel spiflash-info
Manufacturer: Winbond (EFh), model: 40h, size: 2097152 bytes.

$ sudo ./sunxi-fel spiflash-read 0 0x200000 backup-1.bin
# power-cycle, then the second read
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel spiflash-read 0 0x200000 backup-2.bin
$ cmp backup-1.bin backup-2.bin && echo "reads agree"

Copy the backup off this machine before going further. Run spiflash-info first and believe it over this guide: it reads the chip's own JEDEC ID.

Step 2 — Confirm your scope's actual geometry

Read your own dump, don't assume anyone else's
$ python3 fnirsi_splash.py info backup-1.bin
$ python3 fnirsi_splash.py extract backup-1.bin -o stock.png -s 2

Look at stock.png. If it is your scope's real boot logo and the reported geometry is sane, the layout holds on your unit. If it is garbage, stop — do not write. The version string drawn into that image also tells you which firmware you are on.

Step 3 — Make the new splash

Author at exactly the size info reported. Anything else gets letterboxed onto black, or scaled to fill with --stretch. The destination is 16-bit colour: flat colours and clean type survive well, gradients band unless dithered, which the tool does by default.

Build a patched copy — never edit in place
$ python3 fnirsi_splash.py replace backup-1.bin mylogo.png -o patched.bin

changed 4K pages: 19 -> ['0x13000', ... , '0x25000']
calibration region at 0x1FD000: untouched

$ python3 fnirsi_splash.py extract patched.bin -o preview.png -s 2

The tool refuses to emit a file whose calibration region differs from the source, so a successful write here is itself a check.

Step 4 — Write only the block you changed

The only destructive command in this guide
# cut out just the bitmap block — 4K aligned, so it flashes cleanly
$ dd if=patched.bin of=splash-block.bin bs=4096 skip=19 count=19

$ sudo ./sunxi-fel -p spiflash-write 0x13000 splash-block.bin

# power-cycle first — the session is stale after a write
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel spiflash-read 0 0x200000 verify.bin
$ cmp verify.bin patched.bin && echo "flash matches"
Prefer this to a full-chip write

Upstream's instructions write all 2 MiB. Don't. A partial write at 0x13000 never erases the calibration sectors, so the one unrecoverable region is never in play — not even for the milliseconds a full-chip write would have it erased.

The skip and count above are 0x13000 / 4096 = 19. If your block size differs, recompute both from your own info output.

Then clear the FEL stub (§04) and power-cycle. Your logo, then a completely normal boot.

06Full screen, if the banner isn't enough

Done and working: 800 × 480, the whole panel. This is a bigger change than swapping pixels, and the risk is different in kind.

The stock block cannot hold a full-screen image — 77,784 bytes of payload, and the app starts right behind it at 0x27000. But 800 × 480 × 2 = 768,000 bytes fits comfortably in the 1,151,488 bytes the factory leaves empty between the app and calibration. So put the bitmap there and repoint the SPL.

The SPL's memory map, from disassembling it
0x50c   mov r5, 0x80000000       app load address
0x510   mov sb, 0x81000000       splash pixel buffer — 11.5 MB of headroom
0x514   add r7, r5, 0x1b00000    framebuffer at 0x81B00000
0x548   mov r0, #0x13000         bitmap header address  ← instruction immediate
0x75c   .word 0x13028            bitmap pixel address   ← literal

Two words. build-bigsplash.py writes a new eGON.BMP at 0x100000 and patches both, recomputing the BT0 checksum. Seven bytes change in the SPL. Because the SPL centres with (800−w)/2, (480−h)/2, an 800 × 480 image lands at (0,0) with no scaling and no bars.

Bitmap first, SPL last
$ python3 build-bigsplash.py patched.bin mylogo-800x480.png bigsplash.bin

$ dd if=bigsplash.bin of=bmp-new.bin bs=4096 skip=256 count=188
$ dd if=bigsplash.bin of=spl-new.bin bs=4096 count=4

$ sudo ./sunxi-fel -p spiflash-write 0x100000 bmp-new.bin
# power-cycle
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel -p spiflash-write 0x0 spl-new.bin
Order is a safety property, not a preference

Until the SPL is rewritten it still points at 0x13000, so an interruption partway leaves a scope that boots normally. Only the final seven bytes switch it over. Rewriting the SPL is the one step here that can stop the scope booting from SPI — recovery is §04 plus spiflash-write 0 with your backup's SPL.

Do not overwrite the old block at 0x13000

The SPL is not the only consumer of the splash. The scope app draws it again on power-down, and its reference still points at 0x13000. Observed on the bench: after relocating the boot splash, power-off still showed the old image.

So leave the original block in place. Overwrite it and you corrupt the shutdown screen. The consequence of doing this properly is a scope that shows the large splash at boot and the small one at shutdown — matching them would mean finding the app's own reference inside the 0xBCE00 block, which has not been attempted.

08If it goes wrong

Blank screen, but FEL still works

This is the ordinary failure and it is not serious. The SPL or splash block is unhappy; the boot ROM still answers over USB.

Restore just the SPL, or the whole chip
$ dd if=backup-1.bin of=spl-orig.bin bs=4096 count=4
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel -p spiflash-write 0x0 spl-orig.bin

# or, if you must, everything:
$ sudo ./sunxi-fel -p spiflash-write 0 backup-1.bin

Prefer restoring the SPL alone. A full restore rewrites your calibration sectors with a copy that is correct but no longer current, and puts the one irreplaceable region through an erase cycle for no reason.

Every command returns ERROR -7

Power-cycle the scope. A FEL session does not survive a write or an spl. This is the single most common way to lose an hour convincing yourself the scope is dead.

Nothing enumerates at all

The boot ROM enters FEL when it cannot find valid boot media, and the internal card is checked before SPI flash — which is exactly why the stub is your way back in. If USB is silent, suspect the cable, the port, or power before suspecting the flash.

Scope boots but is out of calibration

Restore backup-1.bin in full. If you no longer have it, this is the outcome the whole guide exists to prevent, and there is no clean fix — the values were specific to your hardware.

09What is confirmed, and what isn't

Rewritten after running the whole procedure on a real scope. Several things this guide previously stated with confidence turned out to be wrong.

Confirmed on hardware

SoC 0x1663 (F1C100s) and a Winbond EFh/40h 2,097,152-byte flash, from the chip's own ID rather than from a dump. A 298 × 130 splash replaced and read back byte-identical. A full-screen 800 × 480 splash at 0x100000 with a patched SPL, likewise byte-identical and rendering correctly. FEL entry via the internal card, and the payload that undoes it. The BT0 checksum algorithm, on three different SPL images including one that boots.

Corrected — this guide was wrong

The splash block is not fixed across models. It varies by firmware. See §01.

There is no external SD card slot. The old step 1 told you to insert one. See §03.

0x1FD000 is not calibration alone. The firmware rewrites config there during normal use. See §00.

Still open

  • The A/B display variants. Official firmware ships as -A- and -B- builds for different panels, with a documented symptom of the image shifting left on the wrong one. The splash rendered correctly here with no horizontal offset, at two different geometries — one unit, so evidence, not proof.
  • Why the v3.0 app will not start under upstream's bootloader. It is loaded and jumped to; the screen stays black. Cache coherency was the obvious suspect and disabling both caches changed nothing.
  • The .BMP checksum field. Still matches no sum over any range tried. Left untouched, and a stale value boots — now confirmed twice on hardware.
  • Matching the shutdown splash. The app holds its own reference to 0x13000 somewhere in its 0xBCE00 block. Finding it is the same kind of search that worked on the SPL, but there is no source for v3.0.

Built on the reverse engineering in pecostm32/FNIRSI-1013D-1014D-Hack and pecostm32/FNIRSI_1014D_Firmware, without which none of this would be reachable. The SD-wipe payload is their fnirsi_1014d_startup bootloader with about a hundred bytes changed.

Everything in §00–§08 was executed on a physical FNIRSI 1014D running firmware v3.0 and verified by full-chip read-back. §09 is the part that is still inference.
